The public sequence and the limits on timing

30 December · about 7 am

Arrest on suspicion

Jefferies was arrested in the investigation. He had not been charged or found guilty. The arrest was public information; it did not verify allegations about his character.

31 December · after first print wave

Attorney General’s warning

A public warning addressed the risk of prejudicing a future trial. It came after the first wave; individual receipt before each print deadline is not established.

1 January · timing unknown

Greg Reardon’s tribute

Reardon publicly criticised media character assassination. Its timing relative to each edition’s deadline is not established.

1 January · evening

Release on police bail

Jefferies left custody on bail while the investigation continued. This evening event cannot be assumed to inform the 1 January morning newspapers; bail conditions ended later.

Publication date is not newsroom knowledge. Edition dates do not give exact publication or deadline times. Later events on the same date cannot be treated as knowledge available to a morning editor.

What Leveson later examined

Leveson’s report directly examined the Jefferies coverage in Volume 2, Part F, Chapter 5, section 4 (printed pages 558–564; approximately PDF pages 135–141). It criticised the concentrated character coverage and reckless publication, recorded settlements by eight titles over 40 articles, and found no evidence of Mirror malice. Those findings have distinct legal and ethical scopes.

Richard Wallace later claimed the Mirror received off-record police briefings; Chief Constable Colin Port disputed that account. Leveson could not resolve the conflict. It does not establish the briefings, their contents, or secret incriminating information.

Later drama is a research lead

The 2014 Peter Morgan drama and director Roger Michell’s interviews may help explain how the story was later interpreted. Dramatised scenes and compressed chronology are not historical evidence, are excluded from the eight-article count, and do not establish what editors knew.

The Lost Honour of Christopher Jefferies · two-part ITV drama ↗
Peter Morgan wrote the drama, Roger Michell directed it and Jason Watkins played Jefferies. The producer describes research through interviews with Jefferies. ITV’s contemporaneous report points to an interview with him. A dramatisation is a research lead, not proof that every scene occurred.

How the drama was researched · Roger Michell interview ↗
Michell describes Jefferies’s requested script changes and the use of police interview transcripts. He also identifies an invented scene. The director’s account helps identify leads and dramatic liberties; the original transcript or inquiry record is needed to substantiate a scene.

How to use this guide

  1. Separate the selected headlines from the four added records and from the wider 40-article settlement context.
  2. Place each public event beside its own timing limit before judging what a newsroom could know.
  3. Keep later legal findings, disputed testimony and creative drama in their separate evidential roles.

Missing newsroom records do not prove ignorance. For outlets without contemporaneous records or editor-specific accounts, the public record here does not establish what they possessed, checked or believed.
